## **Computer Ethics**
***

Defined by James Moore (1985): Computer ethics is the analysis of the nature and social impact of computer technology and the corresponding formulation and justification of policies for the ethical use of such technology.

It examines how constantly evolving technology interacts with facts, conceptualizations, policies, and human values.

* Ethics is not merely a separate department or a late-stage overlay ("responsible AI" tacked on at the end).
* Every design choice inherently encodes values based on engineering priorities.
* The Invisibility Factor (Moor, 1985): Building software is compared to building a house. Specifications never cover every low-level detail; programmers constantly make implicit value judgments about what matters, embedding invisible priorities into the final system.

## **The Drivers of Urgency**
***

**Velocity and Pervasiveness**: Computing has shifted from desktop mainframes to mobile devices, wearables, and "Software as a Medical Device."

**Exponential Growth:** Moore's Law (hardware density) paired with rapid scaling in frontier autonomous AI agents (task-horizon doubling times) amplifies direct real-world impacts.

## **Generational Harms & Case Studies**
***

Computing actions directly alter the physical and social world, often through Automated Decision-Making Systems (ADMS).

Key historical examples include the **Therac-25 radiation accidents** (1985–1987) caused by software failure and engineer overconfidence, and **Australia's Robodebt scheme** (2016–2023), an unlawful ADMS debt-recovery system demonstrating the human cost of uncritical automated oversight.

## **Is Technology Neutral?**
***

Is it purely a means to an end, or does it actively shape and dictate the ends?

### **The Pro-Neutrality View**

Value-Neutrality Thesis (VNT): Technological artifacts do not inherently contain, embed, or possess moral values (Joseph C. Pitt, 2000/2014).

Expressed by the common slogan "Guns don't kill people, people kill people." Under this framework, moral responsibility lies entirely with the human user, not the tool itself.

### **Arguments Against VNT**

#### **Kranzberg’s First Law**

"Technology is neither good nor bad; nor is it neutral." (Melvin Kranzberg, 1986).

Technology interacts with the wider social ecology; its deployment produces human, social, and environmental consequences that reach far beyond its immediate technical purpose.

#### **The "Cost of Innocence"**

Roy J. Whelchel (1986) argues that viewing technology merely as a neutral tool assumes technical means have no influence on the chosen ends.

If technology is strictly neutral, engineers become powerless pawns of external decision-makers: "*To be innocent, we must be powerless.*"

#### **"Do Artifacts Have Politics?"**

Langdon Winner (1980) demonstrates that physical designs encode political decisions.

Example: Robert Moses designed Long Island parkway overpass bridges deliberately low so that public transit buses carrying low-income and racial-minority populations could not access Jones Beach.

Technical choices can permanently enforce social, political, and institutional arrangements that cannot simply be "used differently" by an individual user.

## **Ethics vs Law vs Codes**
***

### **Ethical Principles vs Law**
***

Explores the divergence between legality and morality: an action can be legal but unethical, or ethical but illegal.

Ethical principles are broader than laws; they serve to interpret, critique, evaluate existing laws, and propose new legislation.

### **Ethics vs Compliance**
***

**Compliance**: Following rules against an external standard (laws, codes, audit checklists). It is necessary but never sufficient - a system can be fully compliant yet deeply wrong.

**Ethics**: Moral reasoning about what should be done when existing standards or rules are silent or ambiguous. Ethics treats compliance as a starting point, not the destination.

### **Code of Ethics vs Code of Conduct**
***

**Code of Ethics**: Outlines fundamental ideals, virtues, and guiding principles (e.g., "We have respect for the rights and dignity of people").

**Code of Conduct**: Sets out specific behavioral expectations and rules (e.g., "Support colleagues and treat everyone with respect and courtesy").

Both demand a professional commitment to conform to and uphold those shared standards.

### **Major Professional Computing Codes**
***

#### ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct

Expresses the conscience of the computing profession; holds that computing professionals' actions change the world and must support the public good.

Serves as a baseline standard for all computing practitioners, regardless of formal membership.

Structured into four key sections:
1. General Ethical Principles (contribute to society, avoid harm, honesty, fairness, respect privacy, honor confidentiality).
2. Professional Responsibilities (maintain high quality, continuing education, risk assessment).
3. Professional Leadership Principles (guidelines for managing/directing technical work).
4. Compliance with the Code (promoting standards and remediating violations).

##### IEEE Code of Ethics

Holds paramount the safety, health, and welfare of the public; requires prompt disclosure of potential hazards.

Mandates honesty, competence, avoiding conflicts of interest, and treating all people fairly without harassment or discrimination.

#### Australian Computer Society (ACS) Code of Professional Conduct

Emphasizes honesty, trustworthiness, respect for others, and respect for the computing profession.

#### Joint ACM/IEEE-CS Software Engineering Code

Sets out eight foundational principles: Public, Client and Employer, Product, Judgment, Management, Profession, Colleagues, and Self.

## **Ethical Dilemmas**
***

Definition: Situations where competing moral duties, principles, or stakeholder interests clash, and no clear-cut "clean" choice exists.

Classic Examples: Whistleblowing, animal testing in medical/scientific trials.

### **Ethical Dilemmas in Technology**

* Deploying less reliable alpha prototypes into production to iteratively improve systems.
* Balancing free speech versus content moderation for platforms hosting disinformation and misinformation.
* Mass job displacement driven by AI and Automated Decision-Making Systems (ADMS).
* Algorithmic bias and discriminatory decision engines. 
* Decision-making trade-offs in autonomous vehicles during imminent crash scenarios.

### **Technology Ethics vs Science Ethics**

**Technology Ethics**: Guides regulation and oversight, safeguards privacy and confidentiality, and governs responsible creation and deployment.

**Science Ethics**: Centers on honesty, integrity, replication, unbiased methodology, and transparent peer review.

### **Engaging in Ethical Discourse**

Test technical and moral reasoning through thought experiments and dilemma analysis.

Disagree with the argument rather than the person, keep an open mind, maintain confidentiality, and ground claims by citing sources.
