## **Introduction: Digital Tech & Professionalism**
***

Digital systems are everywhere, but unlike traditional professions (medicine, law, civil engineering), computing does not yet have universal licensing bodies.

Insurers are often the first to force accountability - if they refuse to cover liabilities caused by software failures, industry practices must adapt.

Core Shift: Moving beyond basic rules toward understanding who carries moral responsibility when digital systems act.

## **Digital Ethics**
***

Scope: Covers hardware, software, algorithms, industry practices, societal norms, and human users.

Three Levels of Digital Ethics:
* Ethics of Data: Focuses on collection, privacy, transparency, and data analysis.
* Ethics of Algorithms: Focuses on design, algorithmic bias, accountability, and automated decisions.
* Ethics of Practice: Focuses on the responsibilities of developers, managers, and organizations setting digital policies.

Governance Challenges:
* Governance vs. Innovation: The concern that regulations slow down progress (or that fast progress requires dodging rules).
* Principles to Practices: Avoiding "ethics washing" (talking about ethics without enforcing it) and not confusing statements/outputs with genuine ethical outcomes.
* Global Guidance: Software crosses borders and cultures, making universal ethical standards difficult to establish.

## **Moral Responsibility**
***

To be responsible means having an obligation to respond; being morally responsible means responding appropriately to the moral stakes of a situation.

Human-Centric View: Traditional ethics holds that plans, intentions, blame, and punishment only make sense when applied to humans.

Autonomous Agents: Defined as systems capable of changing internal states and transition rules to reach goals without direct human intervention.

Predictability Requirement: A system's predictability depends on past behavior and environment. You cannot deploy an autonomous system responsibly without understanding how likely it is to behave predictably.

## **Meaningful Moral Responsibility & The Moral Gambit**
***

Criteria for Fair Moral Responsibility:
* Justifiable Attribution: It must be fair and logically defensible to ascribe the action to the agent.
* Acceptance: The agent must be capable of knowingly accepting responsibility for their choices.
* Moral Assessment: The agent must be capable of receiving praise or blame as an evaluation of their moral character.

The "Responsibility Gap": When autonomous AI makes an unpredictable, harmful decision, it lacks moral character - so who takes the blame?

The Moral Gambit: A human developer or deployer voluntarily accepts moral responsibility for the system's actions in advance, acknowledging the risks and unpredictability of deployment.
